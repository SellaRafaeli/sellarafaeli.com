## 22 Things From 22 Years of Writing Code

I wrote my first real program in 2000. Since then: an army unit, a university degree, a big startup, my own startup, a dev shop that built software for about seventy organizations, and lots of freelance work in between. Twenty-two years. Here are twenty-two things I believe, in no particular order.

**1. Boring technology is a competitive advantage.** The new thing costs you learning time, missing documentation, and bugs nobody has hit yet. The old thing just works.

**2. Most code is read far more than it's written.** Optimize for the reader. The reader is usually you, six months from now, at night, during an outage.

**3. The best code is no code.** The second-best code is deleted code. Every line is a liability you have to maintain forever.

**4. Every dependency is [some dude's code](/blog/dependency_madness).** Sometimes it's worth it. Know when you're making that trade.

**5. A monolith is fine.** It's more than fine. Split it when the pain is real and specific, not when a conference talk tells you to.

**6. Naming is design.** If you can't name it clearly, you don't understand it yet.

**7. Simple is not easy.** Making something simple is the hardest work in software. Making something complicated is what happens by default.

**8. Data outlives code.** Your schema will still be around long after the framework you chose is forgotten. Design it carefully.

**9. Store money as integers.** I'll keep saying it until I die.

**10. Most performance problems are a missing index or a query in a loop.** Check those before you add a cache, a queue, or a new database.

**11. Ship small, ship often.** A small deploy that breaks is easy to fix. A big deploy that breaks is a weekend.

**12. Logs and backups are boring until the one day they're everything.** Test your restore. Seriously, actually do it.

**13. Estimates are guesses.** Give ranges, say what you don't know, and update them when you learn more. Nobody minds an honest "this is taking longer". Everyone minds a surprise.

**14. Write things down.** Decisions, agreements, the reason you did the weird thing. Memory is a terrible database.

**15. Senior developers are expensive per hour and cheap per feature.**

**16. Users don't care about your stack.** They care whether it works, whether it's fast, and whether it respects their time.

**17. Talk to users.** One conversation with a real user is worth a week of internal debate.

**18. Saying "no" is a technical skill.** Every feature you don't build is time you can spend making the rest better.

**19. Being right is not the goal.** Shipping something good, together, is the goal. I learned that one late, and I'm still learning it.

**20. Take care of your body.** Your brain is attached to it. Sleep, walk, stretch. No one ever wrote better code exhausted.

**21. Find your people.** The developers you meet, help, and learn from over the years will be the best thing your career gives you. (That's a big part of why I started [indydevs](/blog/why_senior_developers_need_each_other).)

**22. It's still magic.** After twenty-two years, I still get a thrill when I type some words into a file and a thing appears on a screen across the world. If you ever stop feeling that, take a break until you do.

### And a bonus one

**23. Lists like this are always incomplete.** Ask me again in another twenty-two years.
